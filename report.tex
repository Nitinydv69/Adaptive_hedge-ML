\section*{\centering Abstract}
I implement from scratch the \Hedge{} algorithm with a fixed learning rate $\eta$ and the parameter-free
\Ada{} algorithm \cite{vanErven2011,deRooij2014}, and compare them on four synthetic loss sequences ($K=10$, $T=5000$, 20 seeds): a stochastic
sequence with a large gap, one with a tiny gap, a piecewise-stationary (``switching'') sequence, and a deterministic sequence that is a worst case for
follow-the-leader. No fixed $\eta$ is good everywhere: $\eta=0.5$ gives regret $\approx 7$ on the stochastic sequence but $\approx 310$ (linear in $T$) on the
alternating one, while the worst-case-optimal $\eta^*$ is safe but $7\times$ worse than \Ada{} on the stochastic sequence ($39.9$ vs.\ $5.5$).
\Ada{} is better than every realisable fixed choice I tried on all three informative sequences, and within about $1.3$ regret units of a hindsight-tuned oracle
(a small but statistically detectable gap) on the two stochastic-type ones. A scaling experiment ($T=500$ to $50\,000$) shows its regret flat on easy data and growing like $\sqrt T$ on the hard sequence.
On the nearly indistinguishable low-gap sequence no algorithm is distinguishable from another, and I explain why.
I trace the behaviour to $\eta_t=\lnK/\Delta_{t-1}$ and verify numerically that $R_T\le 2\Delta_T$.

\section{Introduction}
\paragraph{Motivation.}
Online learning gives guarantees that hold for \emph{every} sequence, without probabilistic assumptions.
In the course this is developed through online gradient descent over continuous decision sets; prediction with expert advice is
the combinatorial counterpart: a learner combines the advice of $K$ experts, and the standard algorithm, \Hedge{}, depends on a learning rate $\eta$.
Too large an $\eta$ makes the algorithm chase noise; too small makes it slow. The theory-optimal value needs the horizon $T$ and
is tuned for the \emph{worst} case, which can be very pessimistic on easy data. The paper \emph{Adaptive Hedge} \cite{vanErven2011} removes this tuning.

\paragraph{Objectives.}
I (i) implement \Hedge{} and \Ada{} from scratch, (ii) compare them with several $\eta$ on stochastic, low-gap and switching sequences, (iii) identify the regimes where
adaptivity helps, and (iv) connect the behaviour to the theory.
Beyond the minimum I add: an $\eta$-sweep with the theoretical bound overlaid; the trajectory $\eta_t$ of \Ada{}; a deterministic worst-case sequence; a scaling study in $T$;
paired confidence intervals; and a numerical check of $R_T\le2\Delta_T$.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Problem setting}
At each round $t=1,\dots,T$ the learner picks $w_t\in\Delta_K$ (the probability simplex), then observes $\ell_t\in[0,1]^K$ and
suffers $h_t=\langle w_t,\ell_t\rangle$. The regret against the best fixed expert is
\[
R_T=\sum_{t=1}^T h_t-\min_k L_{T,k},\qquad L_{T,k}=\sum_{t=1}^T \ell_{t,k}.
\]

\subsection{Hedge}
\Hedge{} plays $w_{t,k}\propto \exp(-\eta L_{t-1,k})$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:hedge}). For every sequence, $R_T\le \frac{\lnK}{\eta}+\frac{\eta T}{8}$, which is minimised by
$\eta^*=\sqrt{8\lnK/T}$ and gives $R_T\le\sqrt{(T/2)\lnK}$. This needs $T$ in advance, and it is a worst-case statement, pessimistic on easy data.

\subsection{AdaHedge}
Define the \emph{mix loss} $m_t=-\frac1{\eta_t}\ln\sum_k w_{t,k}e^{-\eta_t\ell_{t,k}}$ and the \emph{mixability gap}
$\delta_t=h_t-m_t\ge0$; let $\Delta_t=\sum_{s\le t}\delta_s$. \Ada{} uses $\eta_t=\lnK/\Delta_{t-1}$, with $\eta_1=\infty$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ada}).
The gap $\delta_t$ measures how much the learner loses relative to an idealised mix prediction; it is small when the weights are concentrated
(easy data) and large when experts disagree and the leader keeps changing.
The regret is controlled by this data-dependent quantity: with $M_T=\sum_t m_t$ we have $R_T=\Delta_T+(M_T-L^*_T)$, and for non-increasing learning rates $M_T-L^*_T\le\lnK/\eta_T=\Delta_{T-1}$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bound}
R_T\le 2\Delta_T \qquad\text{\cite{deRooij2014} (proof sketch above)},
\end{equation}
and $\Delta_T=O(\sqrt{T\lnK})$ in the worst case \cite{vanErven2011,deRooij2014} (quoted up to constants and lower-order terms). So \Ada{} keeps a worst-case
guarantee of the same order as \Hedge{}$(\eta^*)$ without tuning, and can be far better when $\Delta_T$ is small.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\Hedge{} with fixed $\eta$}\label{alg:hedge}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $L\gets 0\in\mathbb R^K$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \State $w_k\propto\exp(-\eta(L_k-\min_j L_j))$
  \State play $w$; observe $\ell_t$; suffer $h_t=\langle w,\ell_t\rangle$; $L\gets L+\ell_t$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\Ada{}}\label{alg:ada}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $L\gets0$, $\Delta\gets0$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \If{$\Delta=0$} $\eta\gets\infty$; $w\gets$ uniform on $\arg\min_k L_k$
  \Else{} $\eta\gets\lnK/\Delta$; $w_k\propto\exp(-\eta(L_k-\min_jL_j))$
  \EndIf
  \State play $w$; observe $\ell_t$; $h\gets\langle w,\ell_t\rangle$
  \State $m\gets \min_{k:w_k>0}\ell_{t,k}$ if $\eta=\infty$, else $-\frac1\eta\ln\sum_k w_ke^{-\eta\ell_{t,k}}$
  \State $\Delta\gets\Delta+\max(0,h-m)$; $L\gets L+\ell_t$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental methodology}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Implementation}
Both algorithms use NumPy only (\texttt{src/algorithms.py}). The exponent is shifted by the minimum cumulative loss for numerical stability. In \Ada{} the mix loss is
computed with a shift by the minimum loss over the \emph{support} of $w_t$, which is exact for $\eta=\infty$ and avoids underflow for large $\eta$.
(My first version shifted by the minimum over all experts, which gave $\delta_t\approx1$ instead of $0$ in the $\eta=\infty$ phase; I found this with a two-round counterexample and fixed it before the experiments below.)

\subsection{Loss sequences}
All sequences use $K=10$, $T=5000$ and binary losses (except $t=1$ in the last one). They differ in how hard it is to identify and follow the best expert.
\begin{description}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item[Stochastic.] Expert 1 $\sim$ Bernoulli$(0.3)$, the other nine Bernoulli$(0.5)$ (gap $0.2$).
\item[Low gap.] Best expert Bernoulli$(0.49)$, the others $0.5$ (gap $0.01$, below the statistical resolution $\approx1/\sqrt T\approx0.014$).
\item[Adversarial (switching).] Three equal segments; in segment $s$ expert $s$ is Bernoulli$(0.2)$, the others $0.5$. The best expert changes over time, but the sequence is random and oblivious, so it is ``adversarial'' only in that sense. The best \emph{fixed} expert has mean loss $\approx 0.4$.
\item[Alternating (extra).] Deterministic. Experts 1 and 2 alternate between losses 0 and 1 in opposite phase; the other experts always lose 1. The leader flips every round, so a greedy learner loses $\approx1$ per round while the best expert loses $\approx T/2$. This is a worst case for follow-the-leader and for large~$\eta$; Section~\ref{sec:sweep} shows that fixed-$\eta$ \Hedge{} is also close to its own bound here.
\end{description}

\subsection{Protocol and metrics}
20 seeds ($0,\dots,19$) per setting; all algorithms see the \emph{same} sequence for a given seed (paired comparison). \Hedge{} is run with $\eta\in\{0.005,\ \eta^*\approx0.061,\ 0.5\}$ and on a grid of 11 log-spaced values in $[10^{-2.5},10^{0.5}]$; the best grid value per setting serves as a hindsight \emph{oracle} reference (not a realisable algorithm, and optimistic, since it is selected on the same seeds it is evaluated on).
Metrics: regret at time $t$ against the best fixed expert up to $t$, final regret (mean $\pm$ std), cumulative loss, and for \Ada{} the trajectory $\eta_t$ and final gap $\Delta_T$.
Confidence intervals are 95\% $t$-intervals over seeds (19 degrees of freedom).

\subsection{Stochastic (large gap)}
\Hedge{} with $\eta=0.005$ stays near-uniform for thousands of rounds: its regret is still growing at $T$ ($450$). With a fixed $\eta$ the regret on easy data saturates at a level of order $\lnK/\eta$ (the price of learning which expert is best):
for $\eta^*$ it flattens at $\approx40$ after $\approx500$ rounds (compare $\lnK/\eta^*\approx38$). With $\eta=0.5$ and \Ada{} the regret is a small constant ($6.9$ and $5.5$).
\Ada{} is about $7\times$ better than $\eta^*$. \emph{This is the regime where adaptivity helps most.}

\subsection{Low gap}
All methods finish at $52$--$62$ and the differences are smaller than the spread over seeds, so \emph{no algorithm can be declared better here}.
The reason is not the algorithms: the regret is dominated by the comparator. Over the 20 runs the best expert beats the average loss $T/2$
by $69.0$ (pure noise would give $\approx1.54\cdot\sqrt T/2\approx54$ from the minimum of ten Bernoulli$(0.5)$ sums, the rest coming from the true $0.01$ gap), while \Ada{} itself ends $14.7$ \emph{below} $T/2$.
The comparator is ``lucky'' by an amount no online algorithm can match.
To check this directly, Table~\ref{tab:pseudo} reports the \emph{pseudo-regret} against expert~1, the best in expectation: all learners sit at $\approx30$, only modestly below uniform weights ($39$), and indistinguishable from each other. The $\approx23$ difference to the realised regret ($54$) is exactly how much the best realised expert beats expert~1.
So at this gap and horizon, the sequence simply does not contain enough information to identify the best expert, whichever comparator is used.

\subsection{Switching best expert}
\Ada{} ($9.6$) and $\eta=0.5$ ($12.1$) clearly beat $\eta^*$ ($37.7$) and $\eta=0.005$ ($229$). Large $\eta$ is not punished because losses are random with large within-segment gaps.
Regret is measured against the best \emph{fixed} expert, whose loss rate ($\approx0.4$) is worse than the segment-best expert ($0.2$). \Hedge{} and \Ada{} cannot exploit this gap: their weights depend on \emph{cumulative} losses, so they never forget earlier segments and only converge to a good fixed expert; tracking the switching comparator would need a variant such as Fixed Share. The sequence is therefore a weak stress test of adaptivity, which motivates the alternating one.

\subsection{Alternating (deterministic worst case)}
Large $\eta$ fails here: $\eta=0.5$ follows the flipping leader and its regret is $309.6$, close to near-uniform $\eta=0.005$ ($325$).
$\eta^*$ ($64.7$) and \Ada{} ($54.1$) stay below the worst-case bound ($\approx76$). \Ada{} is below every grid value, including the oracle ($63.7$); with a single deterministic sequence there is no seed-to-seed spread and my $\eta$ grid is coarse, so I do not claim this is significant.
The key finding is qualitative: \emph{no single $\eta$ is good both here and on the stochastic sequence}, while \Ada{} is near-best in both.

\subsection{Hedge vs.\ its theoretical bound}\label{sec:sweep}
Figure~\ref{fig:sweeptheory} plots final regret against $\eta$ together with the bound $\lnK/\eta+\eta T/8$. On the alternating sequence the observed curve follows the bound closely on both sides of the minimum (within a factor $\approx2$ over the whole range), so this sequence is nearly a worst case for \emph{every} fixed $\eta$, and the best fixed $\eta$ is $\approx\eta^*$.
On the stochastic and switching sequences the large-$\eta$ branch of the bound is very loose: the observed regret keeps decreasing as $\eta$ grows, and the best fixed $\eta$ is at the edge of the grid.
Thus the minimiser moves from $\eta\approx\eta^*$ to very large $\eta$, and \Ada{} (red line) tracks whichever is right. On low gap the curve is flat: when data is hard to read, $\eta$ barely matters.

\subsection{Scaling with the horizon}
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} varies $T\in\{500,5000,50\,000\}$ for the stochastic (20 seeds; 10 at $T=50\,000$ for runtime) and alternating (single deterministic sequence) settings, with \Hedge{} at $\eta=0.5$ and at the horizon-tuned $\eta^*(T)$.
On the stochastic sequence \Ada{} stays flat ($6.2,\,5.5,\,5.2$) as does $\eta=0.5$ ($7.2,\,6.9,\,7.0$), whereas $\eta^*(T)$ grows like $\sqrt T$ ($14.3,\,39.9,\,122.0$): the worst-case tuning pays a growing price on easy data.
On the alternating sequence, $\eta=0.5$ grows linearly ($34.0,\,309.6,\,3065$; a factor $\approx10$ per decade), while \Ada{} ($17.4,\,54.1,\,170.1$) grows by $\approx3.1\approx\sqrt{10}$ per decade, i.e.\ like $\sqrt T$, as does $\eta^*(T)$ ($20.6,\,64.7,\,204.1$).
This supports the claim that \Ada{} behaves like a horizon-free $\eta^*$ on hard data and like a large $\eta$ on easy data.

Figure~\ref{fig:eta} shows why \Ada{} adapts. On the stochastic sequence $\eta_t$ stops decreasing after $\approx60$ rounds at $\approx0.66$ ($\Delta\approx3.5$): once the weights concentrate on the best expert,
$\delta_t\to0$, $\Delta_t$ stops growing, and the large learning rate is retained, giving constant regret. On the hard sequences the weights never concentrate, $\delta_t$ keeps contributing, and $\eta_t$ decays like $1/\sqrt t$:
a least-squares fit of the log--log median curve on $t\ge500$ gives slope $-0.47$ (low gap) and $-0.50$ (alternating), recovering the scaling $\eta^*\propto1/\sqrt T$. On the switching sequence the slope is $-0.26$: $\eta_t$ sits on a plateau ($\approx1$) until $t\approx3300$, about the start of the third segment, and only then decreases; on the stochastic one the slope is $0.00$.

\subsection{Verification of the regret--gap inequality}
Table~\ref{tab:bound} checks Eq.~\eqref{eq:bound} in every run: it holds in 20/20 runs in all settings. \section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{When does AdaHedge help?} Clearly when the best expert is identifiable (stochastic, switching): it removes the $7\times$ (stochastic) and $4\times$ (switching) overhead of the worst-case tuned $\eta^*$, and the scaling study shows this overhead grows with $T$.
It protects against the failure of large $\eta$ on deterministic hard data ($6\times$ lower regret than $\eta=0.5$ at $T=5000$, and the gap grows linearly with $T$). It gives no gain when the sequence is statistically too hard (low gap).

\paragraph{Negative aspects.}
(a) A hindsight-tuned \Hedge{} is slightly better than \Ada{} on stochastic ($4.2$ vs.\ $5.5$) and switching ($8.3$ vs.\ $9.6$), by a small but statistically detectable margin; this oracle is not realisable and optimistic.
(b) Nothing is distinguishable on low gap.
(c) The ``adversarial'' sequence is oblivious and random; the alternating one is a single hand-built pattern. Adaptive adversaries were not tested, and neither \Hedge{} nor \Ada{} can track a switching comparator (Fixed Share would be needed).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Only $K=10$ and $T\le50\,000$; $K$ was not varied; 20 seeds (10 at $T=50\,000$); the $\eta$-grid is coarse and the oracle optimistic; losses are in $\{0,1\}$ (the algorithms are defined for $[0,1]$, fractional losses were not tested).
The paper's FlipFlop algorithm, interpolating between \Hedge{} with large $\eta$ and \Ada{}, was not implemented. The worst-case bound on $\Delta_T$ is quoted, not re-derived.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Fixed-$\eta$ \Hedge{} forces a trade-off: large $\eta$ is excellent on easy data and has linear regret on the worst case; the theory-optimal $\eta^*$ is safe but slow, and its overhead on easy data grows with $T$. \Ada{} resolves this trade-off in my experiments: it beats every realisable fixed choice on the informative sequences,
stays within a small margin of a hindsight-tuned \Hedge{}, and never needs $T$ or the difficulty in advance. Its learning rate $\eta_t$ and gap $\Delta_T$ give an interpretable measure of that difficulty, consistent with $R_T\le2\Delta_T$.
